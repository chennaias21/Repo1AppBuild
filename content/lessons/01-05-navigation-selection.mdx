## Why this matters

Scrolling through 4,000 rows with a mouse wheel is the clearest sign of an untrained user. This lesson has no new concepts — just about eight keystrokes that are the difference between looking like a beginner and looking experienced. One of them also finds data problems you'd otherwise miss entirely.

## How it works

**The mouse is for pointing at one thing. The keyboard is for moving through data.**

Three families of movement:

| | Move | Select |
|---|---|---|
| **One cell** | arrow keys | `Shift` + arrow |
| **To the edge of the data** | `Ctrl` + arrow | `Ctrl` + `Shift` + arrow |
| **To A1 / last used cell** | `Ctrl` + `Home` / `End` | `Ctrl` + `Shift` + `Home` / `End` |

<Shot id="1.5.1" />

`Ctrl` + arrow travels until the content changes state — filled to blank, or blank to filled. On a full column, `Ctrl`+`↓` lands on the last filled row instantly, whether that's row 12 or row 900,000.

Adding `Shift` selects as it travels. **`Ctrl`+`Shift`+`↓` means "select from here to the bottom of the data"**, and it will save you more time than any other keystroke in this module.

### The gap behaviour is a feature

`Ctrl`+`Shift`+`↓` **stops at the first blank cell.** That sounds like a limitation. It's actually your best data-quality check.

Run `Ctrl`+`↓` down each key column of a new dataset. If the columns stop at different rows, you have missing values — and you've found them before they corrupt a total.

<Callout type="warning">
The flip side: if you *assume* `Ctrl`+`Shift`+`↓` reached the bottom, you can build a formula covering half your data. It returns a plausible number with no error. Use `Ctrl`+`Shift`+`End` when you want the whole dataset regardless of gaps, and `Ctrl`+`↓` deliberately when you're hunting for them.
</Callout>

### Sheets and workbooks

`Ctrl`+`PageDown` and `Ctrl`+`PageUp` move between sheet tabs. With twenty tabs, **right-click the small arrows at the bottom-left** for a jump list.

<Callout type="tip">
Scrolled away and lost your cursor? <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>Backspace</Kbd> scrolls the screen straight back to the active cell. Almost nobody knows this one, and it's useful several times a day.
</Callout>

## Do it

Set up a practice file: type `1` in A2 and `2` in A3, select both, then double-click the small square at the bottom-right of the selection to fill down to A500.

1. Click **A1**. Press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>↓</Kbd>. You land on **A500** — the last filled cell.
2. Press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>↑</Kbd>. Back to A1.
3. Press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>Shift</Kbd>+<Kbd>↓</Kbd>. **A1:A500 selects.** One keystroke instead of a long drag.
4. Press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>Home</Kbd> to clear it.

<Shot id="1.5.2" />

### Create a gap and watch what happens

5. Delete the contents of **A250**.
6. Click A1 and press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>↓</Kbd>. You stop at **A249** — the edge of the filled block.
7. Press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>↓</Kbd> again. It jumps the gap and lands on **A251**.
8. Now press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>Shift</Kbd>+<Kbd>↓</Kbd> from A1. It selects only **A1:A249** — less than half the data.

<Shot id="1.5.3" />

That's the trap and the diagnostic, in the same two keystrokes.

9. From A1, press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>Shift</Kbd>+<Kbd>End</Kbd>. **Now the whole range selects**, gap included, because it respects the used range rather than the filled block.

### Go To and Go To Special

10. Press <Kbd>F5</Kbd>, type `A3000`, press <Kbd>Enter</Kbd>. You're far below the data.
11. Press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>Backspace</Kbd>. The screen snaps back to your active cell.
12. Select A1:A500, press <Kbd>F5</Kbd> → **Special** → **Blanks** → OK. Every blank cell selects at once — the foundation of the cleaning technique in Lesson 7.3.

<Shot id="1.5.4" />

13. Press <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>PageDown</Kbd> and <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>PageUp</Kbd> to move between sheets.

## Shortcuts

<ShortcutTable>

| Shortcut | What it does | When to use it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Ctrl` + `Shift` + `↓` | Selects from here to the bottom of the filled block | Selecting one column of data — but note it stops at gaps | Click E2, press it |
| `Ctrl` + `Shift` + `End` | Selects from here to the last used cell | Selecting a whole dataset regardless of internal gaps | Click A1, press it, then Ctrl+T |
| `Ctrl` + `Backspace` | Scrolls back to the active cell | You scrolled away and can't find your cursor | The rescue key nobody knows |
| `F5` → Special | Selects cells by type — blanks, formulas, errors | Data cleaning, and auditing someone else's file | F5 → Special → Blanks |

</ShortcutTable>

## Watch out

<MistakeTable>

| Mistake | What you see | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Assuming `Ctrl+Shift+↓` reached the bottom** | A total covering half the data, with no error | `Ctrl+Shift+End`, or check with `Ctrl+End` first |
| Drag-selecting long ranges | Slow, and easy to overshoot or miss the last row | `Ctrl+Shift+↓` |
| `Ctrl` + arrow scrolls the screen instead of moving the cursor | The cursor stays put | Scroll Lock is on — press the Scroll Lock key |
| Using the scroll bar on a large file | Slow and imprecise | `F5` and type the address |
| Forgetting multiple sheets are selected | You type in one tab and overwrite eleven others | The title bar shows `[Group]` — click a single tab |

</MistakeTable>

<Callout type="story">
**Nikhil, Operations Analyst.** He was reconciling a 38,000-row logistics export against a summary claiming 41,200 shipments. The numbers wouldn't match and he'd spent an afternoon on it.

He clicked A1 and pressed <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>↓</Kbd>. It stopped at row **12,447**. Pressed it again — the data resumed at 12,449.

There was a blank row where the export had been interrupted and restarted. Every formula in his summary used ranges that had quietly stopped at 12,447.

Total diagnosis time: **under a minute**, using a keystroke he'd learned that morning.
</Callout>

## Practice

<Exercise id="1.5.e1">

<Scenario>
Assess an attendance file's size and completeness before analysing it.
</Scenario>

<Dataset name="Attendance_Register">

Build a practice file: `EmpID` in A1, `Date` in B1, `Status` in C1. Fill A2:A1000 with `E1001`, and C2:C1000 with `Present`. Then **delete the contents of C500 and C501** to simulate missing data.

</Dataset>

<Task>

1. Without scrolling, find the last row of the EmpID column.
2. Without scrolling, find where the Status column first breaks.
3. From C2, select the Status data with one keystroke combination. What range selects?
4. From A1, select the whole dataset with one keystroke combination. What range selects?
5. Select C2:C1000 via the Name Box, then use Go To Special to select the blanks. How many? Read the status bar Count.
6. Press <Kbd>F5</Kbd>, jump to A3000, then return to your active cell without using <Kbd>Ctrl</Kbd>+<Kbd>Home</Kbd>.
7. Compare your answers to steps 3 and 4. Which one would you use before building a pivot table, and why?

</Task>

<Expected>
1. A1000 · 2. `Ctrl+↓` from C1 stops at C499 · 3. **C2:C499** — it stops at the gap · 4. `Ctrl+Shift+End` selects A1:C1000 · 5. 2 blanks · 6. `Ctrl+Backspace`
</Expected>

<Solution>

```
1. Click A1, Ctrl+↓
2. Click C1, Ctrl+↓  → lands on C499
3. Click C2, Ctrl+Shift+↓  → selects C2:C499
4. Click A1, Ctrl+Shift+End  → selects A1:C1000
5. Name Box → C2:C1000 → Enter → F5 → Special → Blanks → OK
6. Ctrl+Backspace
```

</Solution>

<Why>

**Step 3 is the single most common source of silently wrong totals in real workbooks.** It selects 498 of your 999 records and returns a perfectly plausible number — which is exactly why nobody catches it. There's no error, no warning, and the figure looks like a figure.

**Step 7's answer:** `Ctrl+Shift+End` before building anything, because it covers the full used range regardless of gaps. But use `Ctrl+↓` deliberately first, to *find* the gaps — a pivot table built on data with blank rows produces a `(blank)` category and misleading counts. Better still, convert the range to a Table (Lesson 6.1), which removes the whole class of range-selection error.

</Why>
