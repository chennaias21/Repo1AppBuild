## Why this matters

A formula is an instruction. Get one habit right here — referencing cells rather than typing numbers — and your workbooks stay correct as they age. Get it wrong and they quietly go stale, which is how eleven salespeople end up underpaid for four months.

## How it works

Every formula starts with `=`. Excel does whatever comes next.

<FormulaBlock>
```
=100+50          → 150
=B2+B3           → whatever's in B2 plus whatever's in B3
=B2*12           → B2 multiplied by 12
```
</FormulaBlock>

### The rule that separates beginners from everyone else

**Never type a number into a formula if that number lives in a cell.**

`=640000*12` works. `=B2*12` also works — and it keeps working when the salary changes, it tells the reader where the number came from, and it can be audited. Hard-coded numbers are the most common cause of reports that are quietly wrong for months.

### The operators

| Operator | Does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `+` `-` `*` `/` | Arithmetic | `=B2*12` |
| `^` | Power | `=B2^2` |
| `&` | Joins text | `=B2&" "&C2` |
| `=` `<>` `>` `<` `>=` `<=` | Compare — returns TRUE/FALSE | `=B2>50000` |

### Order of operations

Excel does **not** read left to right. It follows a fixed priority:

```
1. ( )        brackets first
2. ^          powers
3. * and /    left to right
4. + and -    left to right
5. &          text joining
6. comparisons
```

So `=2+3*4` is **14**, not 20. And `=(2+3)*4` is **20**.

This matters more than it looks. `=B2+C2/2` means "B2 plus half of C2" — not "half of B2 plus C2". That's the difference between a correct average and a wrong one, and it produces a plausible number either way.

<Callout type="warning">
**Excel never tells you your logic is wrong.** An operator-precedence error returns a number, not an error. Use brackets even when they're optional — `=(B2+C2)/2` says exactly what it means, and formulas get reviewed by people under time pressure.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
`F9` is the debugging tool nobody teaches. Press `F2` to edit, select just one part of the formula with the mouse, press `F9` — Excel replaces it with its result so you can see what that piece actually returns. Press `Esc`, not Enter, to restore the formula.
</Callout>

## Shortcuts

<ShortcutTable>

| Shortcut | What it does | When to use it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `F9` on a selected part of a formula | Evaluates just that piece | A formula returns the wrong number and you need to know which part | Select `B2*C2` → `18000`, then Esc |
| `Ctrl` + `` ` `` | Shows every formula instead of results | Reviewing a sheet you didn't build — spots where someone typed over a formula | The odd cell with no `=` is your suspect |
| `Alt` + `=` | AutoSum | Any column total | Click below the column, press it, Enter |
| `F2` | Edit in place, with coloured reference highlighting | Checking a formula points at the cells you intended | Every formula review |

</ShortcutTable>

## Watch out

<MistakeTable>

| Mistake | What you see | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard-coding a value** | The report goes stale silently when the value changes | Reference a labelled cell |
| Missing brackets | `=B2+C2/2` halves only C2 | `=(B2+C2)/2` |
| Forgetting the `=` | The cell shows text | Every formula starts with `=` |
| Referencing a text cell in arithmetic | `#VALUE!` | Check with `ISNUMBER` (Lesson 2.2) |
| Unmatched brackets | Excel refuses the entry | Excel colour-codes matching pairs as you type |
| A formula referencing its own cell | Circular reference warning | Excel names the cell — fix the reference |

</MistakeTable>

<Callout type="story">
**Vikram, Sales Manager.** Commission for 40 salespeople, built as `=B2*0.05` all the way down — 5% hard-coded into forty formulas.

In April the structure changed to 6%. He updated the ones he remembered and missed eleven.

The error surfaced in the August audit: eleven people had been underpaid for four months. The company paid the arrears, the salespeople lost trust in the payroll process, and Vikram spent a day rebuilding the file.

The correct version puts `6%` in a cell labelled "Commission Rate" and every formula reads `=B2*$F$1`. A rate change becomes a one-cell edit that's impossible to get partially right.

**Any number appearing in more than one formula belongs in a cell.** That's not a style preference — it's the difference between a file that ages well and one that becomes a liability.
</Callout>

## Practice

<Exercise id="3.1.e1">

<Scenario>
Build a monthly payroll calculator for a five-person team.
</Scenario>

<Dataset name="HR_Employee_Master" starter="starter/03-01-formulas.xlsx">

| | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Employee | Basic | Days Worked | Advance |
| **2** | Anil Kumar | 45000 | 22 | 5000 |
| **3** | Meera Nair | 62000 | 20 | 0 |
| **4** | Rahul Shah | 38000 | 24 | 3000 |
| **5** | Sneha Rao | 51000 | 18 | 8000 |
| **6** | Kabir Menon | 47000 | 24 | 0 |

Rates in labelled cells: **H1** `Working Days` = `24` · **H2** `HRA %` = `40%` · **H3** `PF %` = `12%`

</Dataset>

<Task>

1. In E2, calculate earned basic: Basic ÷ Working Days × Days Worked. **Reference H1, don't type 24.**
2. In F2, HRA = Earned Basic × HRA%.
3. In G2, Gross = Earned Basic + HRA. In H5... use **I2** for PF = Earned Basic × PF%.
4. In J2, Net Pay = Gross − PF − Advance. Fill everything down.
5. Change H2 from 40% to 45%. Confirm every row updates. If any row doesn't, what did you do wrong?
6. In L1, total the Net Pay column. In L2, average it.
7. Predict before testing: what does `=B2/H1*C2` give versus `=B2/(H1*C2)`? Try both.

</Task>

<Expected>
E2 = 41,250 · F2 = 16,500 · G2 = 57,750 · I2 = 4,950 · J2 = 47,800 · Total Net Pay ≈ 260,693
</Expected>

<Solution>

```
E2: =B2/$H$1*C2
F2: =E2*$H$2
G2: =E2+F2
I2: =E2*$H$3
J2: =G2-I2-D2
L1: =SUM(J2:J6)
L2: =AVERAGE(J2:J6)
```

</Solution>

<Why>

**Step 7 is the order-of-operations lesson made concrete.** `=B2/H1*C2` divides then multiplies, left to right: 45000 ÷ 24 = 1875, × 22 = **41,250**. Correct — daily rate times days worked.

`=B2/(H1*C2)` does the bracket first: 24 × 22 = 528, then 45000 ÷ 528 = **85.23**. Completely wrong, and it doesn't *look* wrong — it's just a small number in a cell.

That's the danger of precedence errors generally: they produce numbers, not errors. Building the calculation in labelled steps — earned basic, then HRA, then gross — rather than one giant formula means each step is checkable on its own.

**Step 5 is the hard-coding test.** If any rate was typed into a formula, that row quietly keeps the old value. On five rows you'd notice. On a 400-row payroll you wouldn't.

</Why>

</Exercise>
